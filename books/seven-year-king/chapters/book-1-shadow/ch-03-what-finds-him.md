# Chapter 3
## What Finds Him

Monday the kettles are still kettles. Mark is still Mark. The shoulder is a liar: fine when he lifts, wrong when he doesn't. He picks. He packs. He eats the same sandwich. He does not think about Sian's blood on a fig-coloured pillow, except when he does, which is every time the warehouse goes quiet between rollers.

He has had two more nights of almost-dream. Crows. A ford. A man in a coat who did not steam. He woke once with his hand on his own throat, feeling for a pulse as if someone had told him to know it would still be going. He took the hand off. He went to work.

At eleven a man is standing on the loading dock who does not work there.

That is the first wrong thing. Visitors wear lanyards and looks of apology. This one wears a coat that is too good for Trafford Park and no look of apology at all. Grey at the temples, dark elsewhere. Forty-something if you were filling a form. The air around him does not move quite with the rest of the air. He does not steam, not properly, though it is cold enough that Mark's breath is a comic strip.

The man looks at him as if he has been described.

"You," he says. Not loud. The rollers do not care. "A word."

Mark is already coming, chest first. "Mate, you can't—"

The man looks at Mark. Mark stops talking. It is not magic that anyone would put in a film. It is the look you get from a landlord, or a copper, or your da when you are ten. Mark finds something to do with a clipboard that does not need doing.

He could walk back into aisle four. He could drop a kettle. He could, he knows this the way he knew the hi-vis bag on the bus, put this man on the concrete and make a mess. He does not. He goes to the dock edge because curiosity is a hunger too, and he is tired of toast.

Up close the coat smells of cold iron and old rooms. The man's eyes are a colour that refuses to settle. He smiles with only half a mouth, which is a family look Ryan would have recognised, if Ryan were a person this man was allowed to be.

"You opened," the man says. "That can be trained. That is what I do. Not a school. A house. You will hate parts of it. That is the point."

"I don't know you."

"Cairn Vale. You may call me Cairn, or Vale, or nothing. I answer to all three. You opened on Friday in Fallowfield. You left a smell. You gave a girl a nosebleed and a bus a second one. That is sloppy. It is also interesting."

The loading dock. A forklift reversing, beep-beep. The Tesco sign across the ring road. A crow on it, of course a crow. He puts his hands in his hi-vis pockets so they will not do something true.

"I didn't give anyone anything."

"You did. You don't have the words. I have some spare." Cairn glances at the warehouse as if it is a joke they share. "You are not a witch. You are not one of mine, yet. You are a hole. Holes get used. I would rather use you than let the wrong mouth find you first. That is the whole of my kindness. It is not a large kindness."

"Fuck off."

Cairn's smile goes complete, which is worse. "Good. Come anyway. Palatine. The brick pile with the hedge that looks like it pays tax. Nine o'clock. If you don't, someone else will come, and they will not talk. I am talking. Notice."

He turns to go. Then, as if it is an afterthought and it is not: "The dream. The bird and the man. Don't tell me it. I don't want it. I can smell it on you. Keep it. It will keep you."

He walks away across the car park and does not look like a man who has walked across a car park. Mark returns with the clipboard.

"Who was that."

"Sales," he says, which is the first lie of the day, and Mark believes it because sales is a bin too.

---

He does not go at nine.

He goes at twenty past, because arriving on time would be obedience, and he is still a man who stands up a kettle aisle, and because he is going, which is already the yes Cairn had, and what is left is manners.

Palatine Road in the wet. Big brick. Hedges. The kind of house that had money when money was cotton. He has walked past houses like this on buses and felt nothing. Tonight the air in the front garden does not move with the street. A fox looks at him from the drive and decides he is not food yet. The door opens before he knocks.

A woman. Brown skin, black hair in a knot that has given up being neat, a jumper that has been expensive and is now beloved. Bare feet. She looks at him as if he is a delivery she did not order and might keep anyway.

"You're late," she says. "Good. I'm Priya. Don't wipe your feet, the carpet's seen worse. He's in the back."

The hall is warm in a way his house is not allowed to be. No damp. A smell like old blood and polish and someone's dinner that was actually cooked. Photographs on the wall that do not match each other: a mill, a ship, a party in a room with too many candles, a boy who might be Joss later if Joss is a boy who was ever a boy. He does not stop. Priya walks like the house belongs to her and also like she is borrowing it. Both can be true.

The back is a kitchen that has never been in an agency ad. Cairn at the table with a glass of something that is not wine and is not not-wine. Another man at the counter, younger, fair, beautiful in the way of people who have been told. That one looks at him and smiles with all his teeth.

"The hole," the fair one says. "He's smaller than you said."

"He's standing in my kitchen, Drew. Use a name when he has one."

"He hasn't."

"Then don't."

Drew's smile does not change. It is already a collar, if you know how to see collars. He does not, yet. He sees a pretty man in someone else's house being rude, which is ordinary.

Cairn nods at a chair. He does not sit. Cairn does not ask twice. Priya puts a mug in his hand that is tea, actual tea, and the kindness of it is so aggressive he almost laughs.

"You felt someone," Cairn says. "From the inside. You felt a street. You spent, and the spend went further than a body. That is DRAW, though you will not like the verb. Most people have a tank. You have a leak. Leaks are useful. They are also how you die in a week if nobody shows you a tap."

"I want to go home."

"You will. Tonight, even. I am not a kidnapper. I am an offer. Stay out there and the next mouth will not make tea. Come here in the evenings. Learn not to bleed strangers on buses. Eat food that is not a meal deal. In return we take a little, sometimes, and we do not take the rest without asking, until we do, and I will try to say when. That last sentence is the honest one. The others are true as well."

Priya leans on the counter. "He's always like this. It's not you."

"It is a bit him," Drew says.

A door upstairs bangs. Footsteps that want to be heard. The man who comes down is Joss, though nobody has said the name yet: dark hair, a mouth that has been designed for contempt, a shirt that cost more than a week's kettles. He stops on the last step and looks at the unnamed man as if looking were a sport.

"This is the one."

"This is the one," Cairn says.

Joss comes into the kitchen, too close, the way people do when they have never been hit for it. He smells like cold and like something sweet underneath. He reaches out and takes the unnamed man's jaw in his fingers, turns his face, looks at his eyes, his mouth, the pulse in his throat. He could break the fingers. He does not. The not-doing sits in him like a coal.

"He's open," Joss says, to Cairn, as if the man in his hand is a coat. "I can taste it from here. Can I."

"No."

"Spoiling."

"Yes." Cairn's voice does not rise. "He is not a meal. He is a student, if he consents to be, and a guest until then. You will be civil. Civil is a stretch for you; attempt it."

Joss's thumb brushes the unnamed man's lower lip, a little cruelty, a little interest, and then he lets go. "He can attempt not to flinch. We'll see who learns faster."

He goes to the fridge as if nothing happened. The fridge is full of ordinary food. That is the part that will bother him later, on the bus: the milk. The butter. The fact that monsters keep butter.

"What's the take," he says, because class has taught him to ask what things cost.

Cairn looks pleased, which is not the same as kind. "Blood, sometimes. Not tonight. Not until you say. Access, later — the inside of a mind is a room we can enter. You felt that already, in the girl. Power, if you stay long enough to grow any. Protection, which is a word people like. I like useful. You will be useful or you will be a mess I have to mop. I prefer useful."

"And if I say no."

"Then you walk out of my kitchen and I wish you luck I do not have to give." Cairn drinks. The glass is darker than tea. "The luck will not hold. You opened. The city has a nose. I am not the only nose."

Priya's voice is quieter. "You can sleep here if the house you have is a shit house. I'm just saying. No one will touch you in your sleep. That's a house rule. Joss, that's a house rule."

"I heard you the first time," Joss says, to the butter.

He does not sleep there. He finishes the tea because wasting it would be a different poverty. He walks back to the damp carpet in the rain. A crow keeps pace along the hedges for three streets and then cannot be bothered. In his pocket his phone buzzes: Mark, a shift-change, ignore. Under that, a number that was not in his phone before, saved as *Vale*. He did not give Cairn his number. He looks at it until the rain makes a map on the cracked screen, and he does not delete it, and he does not ring his mother either, because he has not rung her in months and a vampire in a kitchen is not the reason to start.

He lies in bed and feels, very clearly, the shape of Joss's fingers on his jaw, and the tea, and the butter, and the fact that he could have broken the fingers and the fact that he did not, and he cannot tell which fact is the one that will matter.

He goes back the next evening. And the next.

That is the recruitment. It does not feel like a movie. It feels like a job with better heating.
