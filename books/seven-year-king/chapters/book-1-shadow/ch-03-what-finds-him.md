# Chapter 3
## What Finds Him

Monday the kettles are still kettles. Mark is still Mark. The shoulder is a liar: fine when he lifts, wrong when he doesn't.

He picks. He packs. He eats the same sandwich. He does not think about Sian's blood on a pillow, except when he does, which is every time the warehouse goes quiet between rollers.

Two more nights of almost-dream. Crows. A ford. A man in a coat who did not steam. He woke once with his hand on his own throat, feeling for a pulse as if someone had told him to know it would still be going. He took the hand off. He went to work.

At eleven a man is standing on the loading dock who does not work there.

That is the first wrong thing. Visitors wear lanyards and looks of apology. This one wears a coat that is too good for Trafford Park and no look of apology at all. Grey at the temples, dark elsewhere. Forty-something if you were filling a form. The air around him does not move quite with the rest of the air. He does not steam, not properly, though it is cold enough that Mark's breath is a comic strip.

The man looks at him as if he has been described.

"You," he says. Not loud. The rollers do not care. "A word."

Mark is already coming, chest first. "Mate, you can't—"

The man looks at Mark. Mark stops talking. It is not magic that anyone would put in a film. It is the look you get from a landlord, or a copper, or your da when you are ten. Mark finds something to do with a clipboard that does not need doing.

He could walk back into aisle four. He could drop a kettle. He could, he knows this the way he knew which box would slip, put this man on the concrete and make a mess. He does not. He goes to the dock edge because curiosity is a hunger too, and he is tired of toast.

Up close the coat smells of cold iron and old rooms. The man's eyes are a colour that refuses to settle. He smiles with only half a mouth.

"You opened," the man says. "Friday. Fallowfield. We smelled you across half the city. That can be trained. That is what I do. Not a school. A house. You will hate parts of it. That is the point."

"I don't know you."

"Cairn Vale. You may call me Cairn, or Vale, or nothing. I answer to all three. You left a smell. Not ours. The other flag. Green. Land. Untrained witch-work walking around with no house on it. That is sloppy."

A forklift reversing, beep-beep. The Tesco sign across the ring road.

"I'm not looking for a house."

"You are looking for a reason your shoulder will not sit and a reason a nurse bled on you when you came. I can give you a better reason than tired. Come and drink a tea. If the tea is a kidnapping you may leave the mug."

He almost laughs. He does not. "When."

"This evening. Palatine Road. The brick one with the green door and the hedge that thinks it is a wall. You will know it. You already do, a little."

Cairn puts nothing in his hand. No card. No threat that would hold up in a court. He looks once more as if measuring a coat, and then he walks off the dock into the rain and does not put his collar up, because the rain is not invited.

Mark says, "Who was that," and does not wait for an answer, because the clipboard has become urgent again.

He works the rest of the shift.

The house is where Cairn said. Victoria Park brick, Palatine, a hedge doing its best. Green door. Warm light in a kitchen window at half five, which is an indecent hour for warmth.

He almost walks past.

He does not.

Cairn opens before he knocks, which he hates, and stands aside like a man who has decided not to pretend this is ordinary.

"Come in. Wipe your feet. The carpet has opinions."

The hall is coats and a radiator that works and photographs in frames of people who might be dead and might be sleeping. The kitchen is the heart. Big table. A radio on low. Butter in a dish. Milk. The ordinary miracle of a fridge that is full.

Priya is at the stove. Dark hair up with a pencil. Mid-twenties. An apron that has seen war. She looks at him the way you look at a delivery you did not order and then she smiles anyway, which is a policy he recognises from Sian and is not the same.

"You're the leak," she says. Then, quieter, almost to the pan: "Christ, you do smell like a park." She looks at him properly. "Sit down before Joss decides you're standing in his doorway. Tea?"

"Please."

"Name."

He shakes his head once. She snorts, not unkind.

"Fine. Be like that. I'm Priya. I cook. I am not the welcome committee. I am the reason the welcome committee doesn't starve."

A man comes in from the hall as if the hall has been waiting to produce him. Drew — he will get the name in a minute — taller than he needs to be, a mouth that has already decided this is his kitchen. Soft jumper. Hard eyes when they want to be. He looks the unnamed man over and does not offer a hand. He offers a chair by pulling it out with his foot.

"Drew," he says. "You're wet. Sit. And put a lid on it, you're making the kitchen smell like wet grass."

Mine is already in the way he says sit. Not a collar yet. A habit.

The unnamed man sits. The tea is good. That bothers him more than it should.

Joss arrives last, which is a choice. Fair, slight, a smile with too many teeth. He looks like he has been handsome as a weapon since he was sixteen and has not seen a reason to stop. He walks the long way around the table and stops behind the unnamed man's chair and puts two fingers under his jaw and turns his face as if checking a horse.

"Pretty," Joss says. "Sloppy. Cairn said sloppy. He didn't say you'd stink of the park."

"Joss." Priya, from the stove, without turning. "House rule."

"I heard you." The fingers stay a second longer than they need. The unnamed man could break them. He can feel that as clearly as he felt the box that would slip. He does not. Joss's thumb brushes his lower lip, a little cruelty, a little interest, and then he lets go.

"He can attempt not to flinch. We'll see who learns faster."

He goes to the fridge as if nothing happened. The fridge is full of ordinary food. That is the part that will bother him later, on the bus: the milk. The butter. The fact that whatever they are, they keep butter.

Cairn leans on the counter with a glass that is darker than tea.

"What's the take," the unnamed man says, because class has taught him to ask what things cost.

Cairn looks pleased, which is not the same as kind.

"Blood, sometimes. Not tonight. Not until you say. Access, later — the inside of a mind is a room we can enter. You felt that already, in the girl. The Green in you is untrained. A witch with no house is someone else's soldier if a land-pack gets there first. We turn that, we get something that lasts. Prize childer. That is the take. That is why I bother. Not kindness. You will be useful or you will be a mess I have to mop. I prefer useful."

"And if I say no."

"Then you walk out of my kitchen and I wish you luck I do not have to give." Cairn drinks. "The luck will not hold. You opened. The city has a nose. I am not the only nose."

Priya's voice is quieter. "You can sleep here if the house you have is a shit house. I'm just saying. No one will touch you in your sleep. That's a house rule. Joss. That's a house rule."

"I heard you the first time," Joss says, to the butter.

Drew watches him over the rim of a mug. "You don't have to decide while the tea's hot. You do have to decide before something else finds the smell. We found it first. That's the kindness."

He does not sleep there. He finishes the tea because wasting it would be a different poverty. He walks back to the damp carpet in the rain. He does not have a word for what they smelled. They do. Green. He heard it and it sat in him like a coat that was not his.

In his pocket his phone buzzes: Mark, a shift-change, ignore. Under that, a number that was not in his phone before, saved as Vale.

He did not give Cairn his number.

He looks at it until the rain makes a map on the cracked screen. He does not delete it. He does not ring his mother either, because he has not rung her in months and a man in a too-good coat is not the reason to start.

He lies in the damp and feels, very clearly, the shape of Joss's fingers on his jaw, and the tea, and the butter, and the fact that he could have broken the fingers and the fact that he did not, and he cannot tell which fact is the one that will matter.

He goes back the next evening.

And the next.

That is the recruitment. It does not feel like a movie. It feels like a job with better heating.
