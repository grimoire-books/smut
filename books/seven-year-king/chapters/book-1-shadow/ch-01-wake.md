# Chapter 1
## The Seed

He wakes.

This is not an achievement. The room does it first: grey at the curtain, a bus going past too close, the particular smell of a rented house that has never quite dried out since whoever lived here before. Manchester in the wet. A Tuesday, if the bins are to be believed.

He is unnamed on the inside of his own head. That is not poetry. He just has not needed the word yet. The alarm on the phone says it is time. The phone is face-down because the face of it is cracked and looking at it first thing is a kind of violence.

There was a dream.

He was a man on wet stone with a bird on his chest. He was the bird. Both, and neither, and the rain going through a mouth that had stopped asking. Heat. A ford. Iron. The rim of a shield under his palm, scarred, the hide wet. A woman he cannot see now, only the pressure of having been seen. Blood that was not a film's blood. Work. A loaf in the wet, of all the stupid things.

It is already going.

He lies still and tries, once, to hold it, and the trying is what lets it out. Like a name you were about to say. Like a joke from last week. What is left is the body: a tightness in the left shoulder that is only sleeping on it, probably; a salt taste; an erection he is too tired to be interested in, which fades as the bus fades, which is a mercy.

He does not write it down. He does not tell the flatmate, who is a shape in the other room and a protein tub on the draining board and a cough. He gets up because the agency will not take a dream as a sick note.

The carpet is damp at the window. It is always damp at the window. He puts last night's jeans on because they are the jeans. Kettle. The mug with the chip. Tea that tastes of the mug. He stands in the kitchen in his socks and watches the small yard, which is a fridge on its side and a bike with no chain and a crow on the back wall.

The crow is only a crow. There are always crows. This one looks at him the way crows look at anyone, which is to say as if he might be food later, and then it goes, and the wall is a wall.

He drinks the tea. He thinks about ringing in and saying he is sick, and he does not, because he is not, and because the rent is the rent. His shoulder complains when he reaches for the tap. He rolls it. It sits. He is a man in a kitchen. That is the whole news.

On the bus he stands because the seats are wet from other people's coats. A girl with a lanyard is doing her face in a phone camera and swearing at the light. A man in a hi-vis is asleep with his mouth open. The windows are steamed. Someone's tinny music is a small war. He holds the rail and feels, for three stops, as if he is the wrong height for himself, as if the air in the bus is a size that does not match the air in his lungs. Then it passes. He puts it down to the tea and the sleeping on the shoulder and the fact that he is twenty-six and still living in a house where the bathroom door does not shut, which would make anyone a bit wrong in the morning.

He does not think: I have been chosen. He would laugh. He does not think: I was a crow. He would like that even less. What he thinks, if he thinks anything, is that he could take the hi-vis man's bag if he wanted, easy, and he does not want to, and the not-wanting is a habit he is proud of in a small, private, unimpressive way. He could hurt people. He does not. That has been true since school, which he does not think about, and it is true on this bus, which he does think about, because the bus is now.

The warehouse is a warehouse. Agency. Pick and pack. A hi-vis of his own, which is a joke he does not share. The supervisor is called Mark and has the face of a man who used to play Sunday league and now plays with other people's hours. "You look like shite," Mark says, which is greeting.

"Cheers."

"Aisle four. Don't drop the kettles. Last lad dropped the kettles."

"I won't drop the kettles."

He does not drop the kettles. He picks. He packs. He eats a meal deal at eleven that is a sandwich, a bag of crisps, a drink that claims to be orange. He sits on the loading dock with his legs hanging and watches the rain miss the city and hit it anyway. There is a crow on the Tesco sign across the ring road. He notices it this time, for half a second, and then Mark shouts, and the afternoon is boxes.

By the time he is back on the bus the dream is a stain, not a picture. Wet stone. A bird. A rim of hide. Heat in the shoulder that is only work. He puts his forehead against the steamed glass and makes a hole in the fog with his heat, and Manchester goes past in smears, and he thinks, without interest, that he should buy a new phone screen, and he thinks, with a little more interest, that he is hungry, and he does not think about the woman at all, because there is nothing left of her to think with. Only a pressure. A seed. It sits in him and does what seeds do, which is nothing you can see.

At the house he takes his shoes off because the carpet is the landlord's religion. The flatmate is out. The protein tub remains. He showers in the rust-smelling water until he is only a body again, and he does not look at himself in the mirror for long, because there is nothing on him worth a story: a mouth, a shoulder, a cock, the ordinary kit. He eats toast standing up. He puts the phone face-down. He does not ring anyone. There is no one whose morning would be improved.

In bed, in the damp, he waits to dream it again, and he does not, not that he can catch. Sleep takes him like a bus taking a stop. The crow on the back wall is gone. The wall does not mind.

He sleeps. The seed does not.
