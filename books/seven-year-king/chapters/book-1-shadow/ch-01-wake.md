# Chapter 1
## The Seed

He wakes.

The room does it first: grey at the curtain, a bus too close to the glass, the smell of a house that has never quite dried out since whoever lived here before. Manchester in the wet. A Tuesday if the bins are to be believed.

The radiator ticks. Next door the radio is still too loud. His palm is on his chest where a bird was.

There was a dream.

He was a man on wet stone with a crow on his ribs. He was the crow. Both, and neither, and rain going through a mouth that had stopped asking. Heat. A ford. Iron. The rim of a shield under his palm, hide-scarred, wet. A woman he cannot see now — only the pressure of having been seen. Three sentences he cannot get back. Blood that was not a film's blood. Work. A loaf in the wet, of all the stupid things.

It is already going.

He lies still and tries, once, to hold it. The trying is what lets it out. Like a name you were about to say. Like a joke from last week. What is left is the body: a tightness in the left shoulder that is only sleeping on it, probably; a salt taste; an erection he is too tired to be interested in, which fades as the bus fades, which is a mercy.

He does not write it down. He does not tell the flatmate, who is a shape through the wall and a protein tub on the draining board and a cough. He gets up because the agency will not take a dream as a sick note.

The carpet is damp at the window. It is always damp at the window. Last night's jeans because they are the jeans. Kettle. The mug with the chip. Tea that tastes of the mug.

He stands in the kitchen in his socks and looks at the small yard: a fridge on its side, a bike with no chain, a crow on the back wall.

The crow is only a crow. There are always crows. This one looks at him the way crows look at anyone, which is to say as if he might be food later, and then it goes, and the wall is a wall.

He drinks the tea. He thinks about ringing in sick. He does not. He is not sick. The rent is the rent. His shoulder complains when he reaches for the tap. He rolls it. It sits. He is a man in a kitchen. That is the whole news.

The phone is face-down because the face of it is cracked and looking at it first thing is a kind of violence. He turns it over. He is late.

On the bus he stands because the seats are wet from other people's coats. A girl with a lanyard does her face in the phone camera and swears at the light. An old man eats a banana as if it has wronged him. The windows are steamed. He makes a hole in the fog with his thumb and Manchester goes past in smears: brick, a closed bookies, a Polish shop already open, a stretch of dual carriageway that thinks it is important.

The dream sits under that like a stone in a shoe. Not a picture anymore. A pressure. Wet stone. A rim of hide. Heat in the shoulder that is only work.

He gets off a stop early by mistake and walks the last of it. Agency warehouse off the park: corrugated, a kettle in a cage of safety posters, the particular smell of cardboard that has been rained on and dried and rained on. Mark is already there. Mark is always already there.

"You look like shite," Mark says, which is greeting.

"Cheers."

"Aisle four. Don't drop the kettles. Last lad dropped the kettles."

"I won't drop the kettles."

He does not drop the kettles.

He picks. He packs. The work is the work: tape, labels, the same box six hundred times until the box is a religion. His shoulder holds. Once, reaching for a high stack, he knows — without a picture, the way you know where your own hand is in the dark — that the stack will slip if he takes the left-hand one. He takes the right. The stack stays. He does not think about it. People guess right all day.

At eleven he eats a meal deal on the loading dock with his legs hanging over the edge. Sandwich, crisps, a drink that claims to be orange. Rain misses the city and hits it anyway. There is a crow on the Tesco sign across the ring road. He notices it this time, for half a second, and then Mark shouts, and the afternoon is boxes.

A lad from the other agency asks if he's going out Friday. He says maybe. He will not. There is no one whose Friday would be improved.

In the locker room he changes out of the hi-vis and catches himself in the cheap mirror. Ordinary. Mid-twenties if you were being kind; a face that has been tired longer than it has been interesting. He looks for a mark on his chest where a bird was. There is nothing. He feels stupid for looking.

The walk to the stop is all ring-road and a hedge full of crisp packets. His shoulder ticks once, the way it ticked in the dream when something in it wanted to be more than a shoulder. Then it is only a shoulder. He rolls it. It sits.

On the bus a man is arguing with a baby, or with the woman holding the baby, it is hard to tell. The baby is winning. He stands and holds the rail and tries, against his own advice, to get the three sentences back.

I will use you.

That one comes. Not in a voice. In the grain of the rail under his palm.

The other two do not. He lets them go before he starts making them up. Made-up would be worse than gone.

By the time he is back on the wet pavement the dream is a stain, not a picture. He puts his forehead on the steamed glass. He thinks, without interest, that he should buy a new phone screen. He thinks, with a little more interest, that he is hungry. He does not think about the woman at all, because there is nothing left of her to think with.

Only a pressure. A seed.

It sits in him and does what seeds do, which is nothing you can see.

At the house he takes his shoes off because the carpet is the landlord's religion. The flatmate is out. The protein tub remains. He showers in the rust-smelling water until he is only a body again. He does not look at himself in the mirror for long. There is nothing on him worth a story: a mouth, a shoulder, a cock, the ordinary kit.

Toast standing up. Phone face-down. He does not ring anyone.

The flatmate comes in at ten with a girl he does not introduce and a bag of shopping that is all protein and bananas. They go into the other room. The wall is thin. Ordinary noises. He puts the pillow over his head and then takes it off because he does not actually mind, he only minds that he is the person listening to other people living.

He thinks about getting his own place. He thinks about the deposit. He thinks about Friday and the lad from the other agency and Sian from receiving who laughed at him last week for dropping a label gun, which was not kettles, and which was funny, and which sits in him in a different drawer from the dream.

He does not know that drawer is about to open.

In bed, in the damp, he waits to dream it again. He does not, not that he can catch. Sleep takes him like a bus taking a stop.

The crow on the back wall is gone. The wall does not mind.

He sleeps.

The seed does not.
