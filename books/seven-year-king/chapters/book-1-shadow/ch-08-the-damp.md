# Chapter 8
## The Damp

He goes back to the rented house on Saturday because a hoodie on a peg is not a whole life, and because the flatmate has texted *you dead* with no question mark.

The carpet is still damp. The protein tub has moved six inches, which is the flatmate's idea of cleaning. The cracked phone on the windowsill looks like a relic from a poorer religion. He stands in the kitchen in his socks and the yard is the fridge and the bike and no crow, and the not-crow is louder than the crow.

"Jesus," the flatmate says, from the sofa, cereal, daylight. "I thought you'd done a runner. Or died. Same difference, I'd have kept the deposit."

"Work."

"Work doesn't give you a hickey, pal."

He had not looked. Drew. Of course. He puts a hand to his neck like a man catching a lie. The flatmate grins, not unkind. Ordinary. A person who keeps butter in a house that does not steam wrong.

"It's nothing."

"It's something. Good. You were getting weird. Talking in your sleep. Birds and rivers. I thought you were on something." The flatmate spoons. "You staying, or is this a visit to your stuff."

He does not know. The mug with the chip. Last night's jeans on the radiator, stiff. A life that is meal deals and Mark and a bathroom door that does not shut. It was enough, three weeks ago. It is still a life. Palatine is a key in his pocket that has started to feel like a tooth.

"Visit," he says, and then, because the flatmate is owed a sentence: "I might be in less."

"Bring beer if you're in at all. And don't do birds at 3 a.m., I have a job."

"Aye."

He packs a bag that is not a moving-out bag: charger, the jeans that are the jeans, a toothbrush that has seen things. He leaves the chip-mug. Leaving it feels like a hostage exchange.

On the bus to Palatine his shoulder sits wrong all the way to the hedge. He does not look up the dream. He does not give the seed a news page.

Priya opens the door before he knocks, sees the bag, and does not make a speech.

"The back room," she says. "It's been a box room. It's a bed now. I put a sheet on. If Drew tells you it's his spare, it isn't."

The back room is small and dry and has a window onto the hedge. He puts the bag down. The key and the bag in the same house. Belonging is a toothbrush in a mug that is not the chip one.

He still goes to the warehouse. He still eats the sandwich on the dock. Work is still work.

The leak is quieter after Cairn's candle, louder after Drew's mouth, always there. A woman in the packing line gets a nosebleed and looks at him, and he goes to aisle four until it stops, and he tells Cairn in the evening, and Cairn says, "Smaller," and, "Soon you stop work or work becomes a crime scene. Not yet. Eat first. Then we'll see about useful."

Joss finds the back room the way Joss finds everything.

"You've moved in," Joss says, in the doorway, blocking, classic. "Stray to pet. Congratulations. Do you want a collar or shall I pick."

"It's a bag."

"It's a bed." Joss looks at the sheet as if it has insulted him. "Don't fuck Drew in here. That's not a rule, that's taste. He'll keep you. Priya will feed you. Cairn will use you. I will still be the one who made you kneel, and you will still not have wrecked me, and that is our little marriage. Sleep well."

He goes. The unnamed man sits on the bed until the heat in his hands goes down. He could have wrecked him in the doorway. He did not.

He unpacks the toothbrush. He does not unpack the charger yet. That is pride, or a fire exit. He cannot tell.

Night in the back room is not like night in the damp. No bus through the wall. No flatmate thump. The house ticks: pipes, a laugh from Drew, Cairn's study door, Priya's radio on low, Irish, something old, not a hymn. He sleeps. He dreams the ford from the wrong height, as a bird, and wakes with his mouth open, not asking. The hedge is a hedge. The bag is on the floor.

In the morning there is toast that is not his toast, and butter, and Joss in a shirt, civil, which is worse, and Cairn saying, "Tonight. You're coming with me. Wear something that isn't a hi-vis. We have a mess that is almost a person."

Priya looks at Cairn as if she might argue and then does not. She puts a jumper on the unnamed man's chair that is not the hoodie.

"Eat," she says. "If he brings you back with a hole, I will put a hole in him."

"Noted," Cairn says, and almost smiles, and the unnamed man eats, because the house is a table, and the bag is in the back room, and the chip-mug is in another life that has not ended, only thinned, like rain.
