# Prologue
## Last Day

He had the afternoon, and he used it the way you use a last piece of bread: without pretending there would be another.

The ford was a working place. Cattle had worn a decision into the stones. He sat with his back to a blackthorn and oiled the blade because that was what you did if you were not going to run.

He did not run.

The whispering had been going on a long time. In the bone. In the breath before a spear-point found its mind. A voice. A pressure. She had been building him. He had let her.

A boy came up from the houses with bread and did not ask. Peat on his mouth. The champion ate. He drank from the river in his palm. Downstream a woman beat cloth on a stone, ordinary as weather, and she was not the one.

Spear in the grass. Shield: hide over wood, rim dark where hands had held it. An axe for when the work got close. He checked the lashings. He did not pray.

His left shoulder sat wrong, as it had for months. He rolled it. It sat. He thought, without heat, that he would not do this tomorrow.

Smoke from the houses. A cow complaining. Someone laughing too loud. He stood. He walked down to the water while there was still a colour you could call afternoon.

She was in it.

Not a pretty ford. Stones showing their backs. She stood in it to the calf with his cloak in her hands, washing it.

His cloak. He was still wearing the tunic. He had not given it to her.

The water went around her shins and took whatever was coming off the cloth — mud, old blood — and kept going.

He stopped on the near bank. He had heard her for years as a voice. This was the first time she was a woman.

She looked up.

Older than the voice had felt. Beautiful in a way that did not ask him to like it. A mouth that already knew the hole that would be in him by morning. She wrung the cloak out and hung it on a thorn. She did not smile.

"You came," she said.

"You told me to."

"I did. Tonight. Then morning. Then you go."

He nodded. A crow went over. He could have turned around. He had not. That was the yes she already had.

"I will use you," she said.

"I know."

"You will die."

"I know that too."

She stepped out of the water. The dress stuck to her thighs. She put her hand on his chest, over the tunic, over the heart that had been listening.

"I want you," she said.

Three sentences. She did not fold the last into the first to make the middle easier. Comfort would have been a kindness and a lie. She did not offer it.

"Tonight," he said.

"Tonight."

She took him off the bank. The grass above the flood-line was flattened where cattle had stood. She sat him down there. She drew the tunic over his head. The air was cold on him. Her mouth was not.

She kissed him like a count. Teeth. His hands found her waist and the wet cloth and then the skin under it. She hiked the dress to her hips and put a knee either side of him and looked at him while she did it.

"Morning. I go."

"Good."

"Go."

"Sleep if you can," she said. "I will watch."

After that she sat with her back to the blackthorn and his head in her lap and watched the dark.

He did not ask her what she was.

Dawn came dirty. A grey that admitted it was morning and had work in it.

She stood. She was dressed. She put the damp cloak around his shoulders.

"The ford," she said. "They will try to take it. If they take it they take the houses. You will not let them. That is the use."

"And then I go."

"And then you go."

She kissed him once, closed-mouthed. Then she was not a woman. A beat of black. A shape on the thorn that might have been a bird. He picked up the spear and the shield and the axe and he went down to the water.

They came at first light from the far bank: not an army, a hunger with shields. Cattle-noise behind him. Children shoved. The ford was the mouth. If the mouth opened, the houses were a stomach.

He stepped into the water to the knee and set the shield.

The first spear came high. He knew where it would be before the man's shoulder finished the throw. Not a picture. A feeling, like knowing where your own hand was in the dark. He was not standing there. The shaft went past. He put his own spear into the thrower's throat and the water took the blood without comment.

Then it was work.

Hide-rim on hide-rim. An axe from the left; he let the shield take it and killed the man in the opening. His shoulder screamed and held. Someone got a blade in along his ribs, shallow and hot. The ford became a press. He could feel which of them would slip, which would hold, which was about to put a spear into the boy with peat still on his mouth who had come too far down with a stick.

He moved into that line and broke it.

That was the one thing. A gap that would have been the houses, closed with his body. He held it. Men died on him and around him and the water went from honest to red. Something in his shoulder opened — not a wound, a wrongness, heat climbing his neck — and he used it because she had said use. The raid broke. They went back into the far willows leaving their dead in the stones.

He was still standing.

Then he was not.

The last one was a boy with a knife who had been pretending to be dead in the shallows. The knife went in under the rim where the hide had pulled. Deep. The champion sat down in the water as if he had chosen sitting. The boy ran. The champion watched him run and did not blame him.

The sky was a flat iron. The spear had gone somewhere. He could hear shouting up the ridge that had the sound of people who would live until winter. That would have to be enough.

A crow dropped onto his chest. Light. Precise. It turned its head and looked at him with an eye that had been a woman's eye a few hours ago.

He tried to say I know.

He did not get it out.

The crow stayed until the breath finished. Then it took off, unhurried.

He wakes in a damp rented house in Manchester with the taste of river-iron in his mouth and a crow still turning behind his eyes.

The radiator ticks. Someone in the next room has the radio on too loud. His shoulder does not hurt. He is not dying. He is late for work.

The dream is already thinning. He lies there a minute anyway, palm on his chest where a bird was, and then he gets up.
