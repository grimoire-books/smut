# Chapter 5
## Priya

The house does not become home in a week. Anyone who says found family arrives like a parcel is selling something.

What happens is smaller. The mug. The hoodie on the peg, which Joss stops wearing after Cairn says his name in a certain way. A key, one Thursday, left on the hall table without a speech. Priya: "If you're going to keep turning up, stop making me get off the sofa." He pockets it as if it might bite. It does not. The lock is just a lock.

He learns the shape of them the way you learn a bus route: by repetition, not by map.

Cairn is the war that will not be named. Phone calls in the study with the door not quite shut. A map on the wall that is Manchester and also not — marks on the Irwell, on a park, on a stretch of the M60 that does not deserve a myth. Cairn looks tired in the way of people who have been tired since before you were born. He teaches in scraps. *Don't pull when you're angry. Don't pull during sex until you can do it without sex. You will fail that one. Fail smaller.* He never says please. He says *again*.

Drew is easy company and a trap with the safety off. He sits too close. He talks about the people he goes out and brings back, the ones who leave smiling and a bit pale, and he says *mine* the way other people say *Tuesday*. He likes the unnamed man's unnamedness. He tries names on him for a joke — *love, hole, stray, pet* — and Priya throws a tea towel and Drew laughs and tries again later. He is not unkind. That is the problem. Kindness and keeping are going to get confused in this house, and Drew is where the confusion will live.

Joss is Joss. He does not spit again. He finds other rooms. A comment about the agency in front of Drew. A game of standing in doorways so the unnamed man has to turn sideways. A hand on the back of the neck, brief, claiming, gone before *kettles* can be used, which is its own meanness — never quite the thing you can stop. The unnamed man files each one. He does not wreck Joss. Joss knows he could. That knowledge is the sport.

Priya is the reason he comes back on the nights Joss has been a sport.

---

She takes him to the shops because the house is out of milk and because she does not trust him, yet, to come back from Palatine with the right kind. Rain. A Tesco that is just a Tesco. She puts yoghurts in the basket with the ferocity of a woman feeding a siege.

"You don't have to tell me anything," she says, in the cereal aisle, which is a place where people tell things anyway. "I'm not Cairn. I'm not going to make you useful. I am going to make you eat."

"I eat."

"Meal deals aren't food, they're a cry for help." She looks at him over a box of tea. "Where are you from. Not tonight. I mean — you talk like you've been a few places and none of them kept you."

He almost gives her a county. He gives her, instead: "North of here. Then here. Agency."

"Parents."

"Mam. Not lately."

She nods as if that is a full form. They queue. The woman in front has a child in the trolley who is kicking the metal, bored, alive, none of their business. Priya looks at the child the way she looks at the unnamed man sometimes: as a thing that should be got home. He looks away. He is not ready for children to be in the story even at the edge.

On the walk back she says, "Joss was a person who got taken badly. That is not an excuse. It is a weather report. If you stay you will see him be worse and you will see him be — not good. Less. I need you to not confuse the less with a debt you owe him."

"I don't owe him."

"You were on your knees in my hall."

"That was me."

"That's what I thought you'd say." She bumps his shoulder with hers, the good shoulder, and the wrong one flares as if jealous. "Come on. Before the milk turns as a protest."

---

The first time he is asked, it is Priya, and it is a kitchen, and it is not a scene anyone would put at the start of a film.

Cairn is out. Joss is out, which is a weather of its own. Drew is upstairs with someone who is laughing, then not laughing, then laughing again. Priya sits him down and rolls her sleeve and looks, for once, unsure.

"I don't need it," she says. "That's a lie. I don't need it from you. I would like it. You can say no. You can say kettles, even though that's his word, you can use it for anyone. I will eat and I will be fine and I will still make you that pasta."

He looks at the inside of her wrist. Ordinary skin. A blue river. The leak in him leans toward it like Joss leaning toward contempt.

"What does it do."

"For me? Fills the tank. For you? You'll feel it. It's not nothing. It's also not the end of you. Cairn would say BIND. I say: I ask, you answer, we both stay in the room."

He could say no. He is not on his knees. There is pasta on the hob. This is the other temperature.

"Aye," he says. "How."

She shows him. No theatre. A small knife that lives in a drawer like a wooden spoon. A line, not a gash. His own wrist, not hers — she is taking, he is giving, the grammar matters. She looks at him the whole time the blood wells, and then she puts her mouth on him, and it is not a kiss and it is not not a kiss. Heat. The pull. He feels her from the inside the way he felt Sian, only Sian had not asked and had not known, and Priya knows, and the knowing is a hand on the back of his neck that is not Joss's. He gets hard. He does not hide it. She does not use it. She drinks, slow, and stops before he would have asked her to stop, and licks the line once, and it closes more than a cut should, and she sits back with her mouth red and her eyes wet, not from sad, from full.

"Alright?" she says.

"Aye."

"Don't say aye if it's no."

"It's aye." He is shaking a bit, like after a fight he did not have. "You're — that's what you are."

"Vampire. Drew's word. It's fine. It's a word." She washes the knife. She puts it away with the spoons. "You taste like a storm. That's not a line. Cairn will want to know. I will tell him you said yes. I will not tell him you were hard, because that is yours."

He laughs, once, wrecked. "Thanks."

"Pasta's ready. Don't faint on my clean floor."

They eat. Drew's guest comes down, flushed, a bit pale, smiling, and Drew waves from the stairs, mine-mine-mine in the wave, and the guest goes out into Palatine as if they have been to a good party. Priya watches the door after, and the watch is not cruel, and it is not free. The unnamed man files that too.

He stays late. He does not sleep there. The key is in his pocket. On the step, Priya says, "You can be ours without being his. That's the thing I'm trying to build. It might not work. I'm building it anyway."

He does not know how to answer that, so he doesn't. He walks. The crow is on the hedge. He almost tells it to fuck off. He does not. He nods, which is worse, a man nodding at a bird in Palatine, and the bird does not care, and he goes back to the damp house and the flatmate says, "You're never in," and he says, "Work," which is no longer the whole truth, and he sleeps, and the seed and the leak and the new little absence in his wrist all lie down together like animals that have not decided if they are a pack.

In the morning Mark says he looks like shite, and he says cheers, and he does not drop the kettles, and the word *ours* sits in him all day, not a collar yet, a mug, a key, a woman who stopped before she had to.

It is not love. It is not not. It is Thursday, and pasta, and a bus, and a house that has started to know the shape of his feet in the hall.
